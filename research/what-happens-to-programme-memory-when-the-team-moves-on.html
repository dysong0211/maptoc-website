Staff turnover and the loss of program memory

Knowledge continuity requires responsibility for handover and access, alongside written records. An archive cannot preserve every form of tacit expertise.

Evidence & Evaluation · Editorial briefSecondary synthesis · Not peer reviewed · Sources credited below

Key takeaway

Knowledge continuity requires responsibility for handover and access, alongside written records. An archive cannot preserve every form of tacit expertise.

Evidence and context

A useful final report should allow a new reader to understand both what was achieved and what remains unresolved. Program memory includes the reasons behind decisions, the assumptions that changed and the responsibilities that continue after a grant ends.

The UN's knowledge-management review describes losses when expertise leaves and difficulties finding knowledge across fragmented repositories. Separately, GEF's portfolio assessment distinguishes satisfactory outcomes at completion from the likelihood that benefits will endure. Neither finding supports a claim that staff stop caring when programs close. UN knowledge-management review, GEF portfolio assessment

My proposed standard for program handover is simple: another person should be able to establish what the team tried, what changed, what evidence was available and what still needs attention.

That means retaining more than a list of achievements. A useful handover might explain why a delivery model changed, which partner will maintain a service, which risk remains open and when a promising result needs another check.

The system also needs an end-of-program arrangement. Who can access the record after funding ends? Who can correct it? Which sensitive material should be restricted? What evidence must remain available independently of a subscription?

Those questions are part of preserving institutional memory. A record has lasting value when the next team can understand it, trust its provenance and use it responsibly.

Implications for research and practice

Knowledge continuity requires responsibility for handover and access, alongside written records. An archive cannot preserve every form of tacit expertise.

Scope and limitations

This is a selective narrative brief based on the linked publications, not a systematic review or a new empirical study. Illustrative scenarios and practice implications are editorial analysis. Findings should be interpreted within the settings, methods and observation periods of their original sources.

The hidden burden of repeated reporting

Reporting burden should be measured as work performed, not inferred from the number of reports. Common formats may reduce translation work without resolving every information request.

Evidence & Evaluation · Editorial briefSecondary synthesis · Not peer reviewed · Sources credited below

Key takeaway

Reporting burden should be measured as work performed, not inferred from the number of reports. Common formats may reduce translation work without resolving every information request.

Evidence and context

An illustrative scenario is a program team answering the same underlying question for a donor update, a management review, a partner meeting and an evaluation request. The audiences differ, but the source material overlaps. The opportunity for improvement lies in how much of that work can be reused safely.

The scale of reporting is documented. A UN inspection recorded 3,000 WHO donor reports for 2016. That is a historical count, not a judgement that all those reports were unnecessary. A later harmonisation pilot found mixed results: some feedback indicated time savings, while other reports took the same time or longer. JIU, Annex II, GPPi's 2018 pilot review

The lesson for a product team is practical. A simpler template may help, but the work before the template also matters: finding the right source, checking its date, resolving conflicting versions and asking a colleague what a sentence meant.

Reuse must also preserve boundaries. A record approved for one purpose may need updated context or different access permissions for another. Repeating a claim without checking its relevance would create a different kind of waste.

Implications for research and practice

Reporting burden should be measured as work performed, not inferred from the number of reports. Common formats may reduce translation work without resolving every information request.

Scope and limitations

This is a selective narrative brief based on the linked publications, not a systematic review or a new empirical study. Illustrative scenarios and practice implications are editorial analysis. Findings should be interpreted within the settings, methods and observation periods of their original sources.

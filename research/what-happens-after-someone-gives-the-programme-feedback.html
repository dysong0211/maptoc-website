Closing the loop on program feedback

Feedback mechanisms need information and a meaningful response pathway. Effects from a particular accountability intervention cannot be assumed elsewhere.

Evidence & Evaluation · Editorial briefSecondary synthesis · Not peer reviewed · Sources credited below

Key takeaway

Feedback mechanisms need information and a meaningful response pathway. Effects from a particular accountability intervention cannot be assumed elsewhere.

Evidence and context

Collecting feedback is an invitation to explain what will happen next. A useful process should make it possible to follow a concern from the person who raised it to a response, a decision or an explicit explanation that it remains unresolved.

Research in Uganda offers a specific example of why the design of an accountability process matters. Björkman Nyqvist, de Walque and Svensson found sustained benefits from a community-monitoring intervention combining participation with performance information. A lower-cost intervention encouraging participation without that information did not improve care quality or health outcomes. These findings concern those interventions and settings. American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2017

The broader principle I draw is that recording a concern should be connected to a usable response mechanism. A program team needs to know who can investigate, which evidence would help and how the person raising the issue will hear back.

An illustrative scenario is a participant reporting that a service is regularly unavailable. Logging the message is one step. Checking the pattern, identifying responsibility and explaining the response are separate steps that deserve a place in the record.

The question for a reviewer would then be concrete: was the concern heard, what was done with it and what changed afterwards? That is a useful standard against which to assess feedback practice.

Implications for research and practice

Feedback mechanisms need information and a meaningful response pathway. Effects from a particular accountability intervention cannot be assumed elsewhere.

Scope and limitations

This is a selective narrative brief based on the linked publications, not a systematic review or a new empirical study. Illustrative scenarios and practice implications are editorial analysis. Findings should be interpreted within the settings, methods and observation periods of their original sources.
